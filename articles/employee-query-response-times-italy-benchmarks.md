# Response times for employee queries in Italy: how to set realistic expectations

> How to define first response and resolution for Italian HR tickets, which query types differ, and how partners can agree service levels that hold up.

Global EOR and payroll platforms often set a single service level for employee queries across all countries. In Italy that approach breaks down fast. The reason is not slow people. It is the mix of query types, the role of third parties, and the local calendar.

This article explains how to separate first response from resolution, which query types need different targets, and what a partnership team should agree before launch.

## First response and resolution are different promises

First response means a human has read the ticket and told the employee what happens next. Resolution means the issue is closed and the employee has what they asked for.

Most disputes about service levels come from mixing the two. A platform promises fast resolution and gets a fast acknowledgement. The employee feels ignored. Or the local team resolves a hard case well, but nobody confirmed receipt, so the employee wrote three more times.

Set both targets separately. Measure both separately. Report both separately.

## First response is a function of staffing and language

First response is the part you can control. It depends on coverage hours, ticket routing and language.

Italian employees expect to write in Italian. A ticket that arrives in English from a platform queue, then gets translated, then answered, loses time at every step. A local team that reads the original message answers faster and makes fewer mistakes.

Time zones matter too. If your platform support sits in another region, an Italian employee may write in the morning and receive nothing until the afternoon. Agree which hours count toward the target. Business hours in Italy are the sensible default.

A realistic first-response target is one that a small local team can meet on a normal day and on a busy payroll day. If it only holds on quiet days, it is the wrong target.

## Resolution depends on the query type

Not all tickets are equal. Group them before you set any target.

**Simple information requests.** Payslip explanations, leave balances, document copies, contract clarifications. These sit with the local team and can usually be closed in the same conversation. Resolution should be close to first response.

**Payroll corrections.** An employee spots an error in a payslip. The fix may need a recalculation, a review of inputs from the client, and sometimes a corrective entry in the next cycle. Resolution here follows the payroll calendar, not the clock. Tell the employee which cycle the fix will land in.

**Requests that need the client.** Leave approvals, role changes, expense queries. The EOR cannot close these alone. Resolution time includes the client's response time. Show that waiting time separately in reporting, or the EOR will be blamed for delays it does not own.

**Requests that depend on public bodies.** Social security records, tax certificates, benefit claims. The local team can submit and follow up, but the authority sets the pace. Promise a status update rhythm, not a closing date.

**Immigration and relocation questions.** These often run on external appointments and document checks. Treat them as cases with milestones, not as tickets with a deadline.

## The Italian calendar affects everything

Payroll cut-offs create predictable peaks. Query volume rises when payslips are released. Staff capacity should follow that rhythm.

Holiday periods also matter. Italy has long summer closures in many sectors, and public offices and client contacts may be slow to respond. Plan for it. Tell employees in advance when responses from third parties will take longer.

Collective agreements add another layer. The same question can have a different answer depending on the sector contract that applies. The local team needs time to check the right source. A fast wrong answer costs more than a slightly slower correct one.

## What to put in the partnership agreement

Keep the service terms short and testable.

- Define first response and resolution in plain words.
- Name the query categories and the target logic for each.
- State which hours and which calendar count.
- Say who owns each status, including waiting for the client and waiting for an authority.
- Agree an escalation path for tickets that stall.
- Agree how misrouted tickets are counted.
- Decide how often you review the numbers together.

The last point matters most. Benchmarks are only useful when both sides look at the same data. Start with a pilot period, collect real ticket data by category, and then fix the targets. Targets set before you have data tend to be either too loose to matter or too tight to meet.

## What employees actually notice

Employees rarely compare your numbers with a benchmark. They notice three things. Did someone answer quickly in their language? Did they know what would happen next? Did the promised update arrive?

A fast acknowledgement with a clear next step and a reliable follow-up beats a rushed answer that needs correcting. Build the process around that.

## Reporting that keeps both sides honest

Share a simple report each cycle. Show first response and resolution by category. Show how long tickets waited on the client, on an authority, or on the employee. Show the tickets that reopened. Reopened tickets reveal weak first answers faster than any satisfaction score.

Keep the report small. A long dashboard nobody reads does not improve service.
