# English-only vs bilingual support for Italian employees: what changes in resolution time and satisfaction

> Why English-only support slows resolution and lowers satisfaction for Italian employees on global EOR and payroll platforms, and what bilingual support changes.

Most global EOR and payroll platforms run support in English. It is efficient for the platform. For an employee in Italy, it often is not.

This article looks at how the language of support affects two measures your team already tracks: time to resolution and employee satisfaction. It makes no claims about specific percentages. It describes the mechanisms, so you can test them against your own ticket data.

## Where English-only support breaks down

An Italian employee writes to support about a payslip. The question is rarely simple. It may concern a deduction, a contribution line, a leave balance or a bonus treated under the national collective agreement. These topics come with Italian terms that have no clean English equivalent.

The employee has two options. They can write in English and risk describing the problem imprecisely. Or they can write in Italian and wait for someone to translate it.

Both options add friction. The first produces vague tickets. The agent replies with a clarifying question. The employee replies. The agent asks again. Each round trip adds delay.

The second option adds a translation step before anyone starts work. The ticket may sit in a queue until someone with the right language skills picks it up.

## Why resolution time suffers

Resolution time is not only about how fast an agent replies. It is about how many exchanges the case needs.

In a payroll case, a precise question saves time. When an employee can name the exact line on a payslip in the language it was printed, the agent can find it at once. When the employee has to paraphrase it in a second language, the agent must guess which line is meant.

Three patterns tend to show up in English-only support for Italian staff:

- More clarifying questions per ticket.
- More reopened tickets, because the first answer addressed a slightly different question.
- More escalations to a local expert who can read the original document.

Each of these lengthens the case. None of them shows up as a language problem in the dashboard. They show up as complexity, or as agent performance. This hides the real cause.

## Why satisfaction suffers

Satisfaction scores reflect more than the outcome. They reflect how the employee felt during the exchange.

An employee who needs to ask about their pay is already under some stress. Being asked to explain a technical matter in a foreign language adds to it. Even confident English speakers often prefer their own language for matters involving money, contracts and legal rights.

There is also a trust effect. When a reply uses the wrong term for an Italian concept, the employee notices. They may not know whether the answer is correct. Doubt about the answer becomes doubt about the employer of record, and by extension about the company that hired through it.

For your client, this matters. The employee does not separate your platform from their employer. A poor support experience reflects on both.

## What bilingual support changes

Bilingual support means the employee can write in Italian and get an answer in Italian, from someone who knows the Italian rules. That last part matters. A translation layer alone does not fix the problem, because the agent still needs to understand the subject.

When both conditions are met, the effects are practical:

- Tickets arrive with the right terms, so fewer clarifying questions are needed.
- Answers cite the correct contract and payslip terminology, so fewer tickets are reopened.
- Fewer cases need escalation, because the first agent can read the source document.
- The employee feels understood, which lifts satisfaction even when the answer is not the one they hoped for.

## What this means for partnership and operations teams

You do not need to rebuild your support team to test this. Start with your own data.

Split Italian tickets from the rest. Compare the number of exchanges per case, the reopen rate and the satisfaction score. If Italian cases show more back and forth, language is a likely contributor.

Then look at the ticket categories. Payslip queries, contribution questions and leave balances are the most language-sensitive. These are the ones to route to Italian-speaking support first.

A full bilingual desk is one option. Another is a partner who handles Italian-language first-line support and feeds structured case data back to your platform. This keeps your own tooling in place. It also keeps your reporting complete, since the partner's cases appear in your system.

The key design point is the data exchange. If the partner works outside your ticketing system, you lose visibility. If the partner works through a defined exchange of case data, you keep it.

## A practical way to start

Pick one ticket category. Route it to Italian-language support for a limited period. Compare the exchanges per case and the satisfaction score against the same category before the change. Keep the test small enough to reverse.

The result will tell you whether language is a real driver in your Italian caseload, or whether the delay lies elsewhere.
